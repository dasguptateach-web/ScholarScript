Introduction

Sherman Alexie's "Crow Testament" is a masterclass in compression and allegory. In just seven brief stanzas, the Spokane-Coeur d'Alene poet condenses centuries of Indigenous trauma—from biblical fratricide to reservation poverty—into the recurring figure of Crow. Alexie uses Crow as a trickster-witness, a survivor who watches, suffers, and comments with bitter, ironic humor. The poem is not simply a lament; it is a testament—a legal and spiritual document of what was taken and what endures. Alexie draws on biblical allusions to indict the very religious framework used to justify colonization, while celebrating Native resilience through Crow's stubborn refusal to disappear.

### Complete Text
Cain lifts Crow, that heavy black bird  
and strikes down Abel.  
### Damn, says Crow, I guess
this is just the beginning.  

The white man, disguised  
as a falcon, swoops in  
and yet again steals a salmon  
from Crow's talons.  
Damn, says Crow, if I could swim  
I would have fled this country years ago.  

### The Crow God as depicted
in all of the reliable Crow bibles  
looks exactly like a Crow.  
Damn, says Crow, this makes it  
so much easier to worship myself.  

Among the ashes of Jericho,  
Crow sacrifices  
his firstborn son.  
Damn, says Crow, a million nests  
are soaked with blood.  

### When Crows fight Crows
the sky fills with beaks and talons.  
Damn, says Crow, it's raining feathers.  

Crow flies around the reservation  
and collects empty beer bottles  
but they are so heavy  
he can only carry one at a time.  
So, one by one, he returns them  
but gets only five cents a bottle.  
Damn, says Crow, redemption is not easy.  

Crow rides a pale horse  
into a crowded powwow  
but none of the Indian panic.  
### Damn, says Crow, I guess
they already live near the end of the world.  

### Stanza-by-Stanza Analysis with Hindi & Bengali Translations
English:  
Cain lifts Crow, that heavy black bird / and strikes down Abel. / Damn, says Crow, I guess / this is just the beginning.  

Hindi:  
कैन ने कौवे को उठाया, वह भारी काला पक्षी / और एबेल को मार गिराया। / धिक्कार है, कहता है कौवा, मुझे लगता है / यह तो बस शुरुआत है।  

Bengali:  
কেইন কাককে তুলে নিল, সেই ভারী কালো পাখি / আর আবেলকে আঘাত করে ফelled করল। / ধিক, বলে কাক, আমার মনে হয় / এ তো সবে শুরু।  

**Key Point:** Alexie places Crow inside the first murder in Western mythology. Crow is not merely a witness but the weapon itself—Indigenous peoples weaponized in conflicts between colonizers, yet also victims of the original fratricide that sets the pattern for all colonial violence.

English:

The white man, disguised / as a falcon, swoops in / and yet again steals a salmon / from Crow's talons. / Damn, says Crow, if I could swim / I would have fled this country years ago.

Hindi:  
गोरा आदमी, बाज़ का भेस धरकर / झपट्टा मारता है / और फिर से सैल्मन चुरा लेता है / कौवे के पंजों से। / धिक्कार है, कहता है कौवा, अगर मैं तैर पाता / तो बरसों पहले इस देश से भाग जाता।  

Bengali:  
সাদা লোক, বাজপাখির ছদ্মবেশে / ঝাঁপিয়ে পড়ে / আর আবারও স্যামন চুরি করে / কাকের নখর থেকে। / ধিক, বলে কাক, যদি সাঁতার জানতাম / তাহলে বহু বছর আগেই এই দেশ ছেড়ে পালাতাম।  

**Key Point:** The falcon—a predator of crows—represents white settlers. The salmon is both literal food source and cultural sustenance. Crow's inability to "swim" (escape) is a grim recognition of trapped indigeneity. The repeated theft signals the systematic nature of dispossession.

English:

Hindi:  
कौवा भगवान, जैसा दर्शाया गया है / सभी विश्वसनीय कौवा बाइबलों में / बिल्कुल कौवे जैसा दिखता है। / धिक्कार है, कहता है कौवा, इससे तो / खुद की पूजा करना और भी आसान हो जाता है।  

Bengali:  
কাক ঈশ্বর, যেমন চিত্রিত / সব নির্ভরযোগ্য কাক বাইবেলে / দেখতে হুবহু কাকের মতো। / ধিক, বলে কাক, এতে তো / নিজের পূজা করা আরও সহজ হয়ে যায়।  

**Key Point:** This is the poem's sharpest theological move. Alexie suggests that all gods are made in the image of their worshippers—and that Indigenous spirituality, grounded in the natural world, was never inferior, only different. The irony cuts against Christian missionary logic.

English:

Among the ashes of Jericho, / Crow sacrifices / his firstborn son. / Damn, says Crow, a million nests / are soaked with blood.

Hindi:  
यरीहो की राख के बीच, / कौवा बलिदान करता है / अपने पहलौठे बेटे का। / धिक्कार है, कहता है कौवा, दस लाख घोंसले / खून से भीगे हुए हैं।  

Bengali:  
জেরিকোর ছাইয়ের মধ্যে, / কাক বলি দেয় / তার প্রথমজাত পুত্রকে। / ধিক, বলে কাক, দশ লক্ষ বাসা / রক্তে ভিজে গেছে।  

**Key Point:** Jericho's walls fell through divine violence; here, Crow reenacts Abraham's near-sacrifice of Isaac—but without the reprieve. Native sons died in colonial wars, and the "million nests" evoke countless families destroyed across generations.

English:

When Crows fight Crows / the sky fills with beaks and talons. / Damn, says Crow, it's raining feathers.

Hindi:  
जब कौवे कौवों से लड़ते हैं / आसमान चोंचों और पंजों से भर जाता है। / धिक्कार है, कहता है कौवा, पंखों की बारिश हो रही है।  

Bengali:  
যখন কাকেরা কাকেদের সাথে লড়ে / আকাশ ভরে যায় ঠোঁট আর নখরে। / ধিক, বলে কাক, পালক পড়ছে বৃষ্টির মতো।  

**Key Point:** This is internal conflict—Native against Native, whether tribal warfare or the self-destruction of alcoholism and lateral violence born of oppression. Crow's dark humor ("raining feathers") masks profound grief.

English:

Crow flies around the reservation / and collects empty beer bottles / but they are so heavy / he can only carry one at a time. / So, one by one, he returns them / but gets only five cents a bottle. / Damn, says Crow, redemption is not easy.

Hindi:  
कौवा रिज़र्वेशन के चारों ओर उड़ता है / और खाली बियर की बोतलें इकट्ठा करता है / पर वे इतनी भारी हैं / कि वह एक बार में एक ही उठा सकता है। / तो, एक-एक करके, वह उन्हें लौटाता है / पर मिलता है सिर्फ पाँच सेंट प्रति बोतल। / धिक्कार है, कहता है कौवा, मुक्ति आसान नहीं है।  

Bengali:  
কাক রিজার্ভেশনের চারপাশে ওড়ে / আর খালি বিয়ারের বোতল কুড়োয় / কিন্তু সেগুলো এত ভারী / যে একবারে একটা ছাড়া নিতে পারে না। / তাই, একে একে, সে সেগুলো ফেরত দেয় / কিন্তু পায় মাত্র পাঁচ সেন্ট প্রতি বোতল। / ধিক, বলে কাক, মুক্তি সহজ নয়।  

**Key Point:** This is the poem's most direct contemporary image. Beer bottles signal alcoholism's grip on reservations; five cents per bottle is a pittance. "Redemption" carries double meaning—Christian salvation and economic survival. Neither comes easily.

English:

Crow rides a pale horse / into a crowded powwow / but none of the Indian panic. / Damn, says Crow, I guess / they already live near the end of the world.

Hindi:  
कौवा एक पीली घोड़ी पर सवार होकर / एक भरे पूर्वजों के मेले में आता है / पर कोई भी भारतीय नहीं डरता। / धिक्कार है, कहता है कौवा, मुझे लगता है / वे तो पहले से ही दुनिया के अंत के पास रहते हैं।  

Bengali:  
কাক একটা ফ্যাকাশে ঘোড়ায় চড়ে / ভিড়ভাট্টা পাওয়াওয়াতে ঢোকে / কিন্তু কোনো ভারতীয় আতঙ্কিত হয় না। / ধিক, বলে কাক, আমার মনে হয় / তারা তো আগেই পৃথিবীর শেষের কাছেই বাস করে।  

**Key Point:** The pale horse is Death from Revelation. Crow expects panic but finds none—because Native communities have already survived apocalypse. The poem ends not with despair but with a terrible, weary resilience: the end of the world is not a future event but a lived condition.

### Key Points for Study

**Symbolism:** Crow represents Indigenous peoples—trickster, survivor, witness. The falcon is the white colonizer. The pale horse is death, but also the revelation that Native communities have already endured the worst.

**Structure:** Seven stanzas mirror the seven days of creation (and the seven seals of Revelation). Each ends with "Damn, says Crow," a refrain that functions as both lament and defiant signature.

**Tone:** Ironic, bitter, darkly humorous. Alexie refuses sentimentality; Crow's wit is a survival mechanism.

**Theme:** Colonial trauma, religious hypocrisy, cultural erasure, and—crucially—resilience. Crow does not vanish. He rides on.

**Historical Context:** Published in First Indian on the Moon (1993), the poem emerged from the Native American Renaissance, responding to centuries of dispossession and the ongoing crises of reservation life.

**Central Insight:** Alexie does not simply compare Native suffering to biblical suffering. He suggests that the Bible's violence—fratricide, conquest, sacrifice—was the template for colonization. Crow's testament is the counter-narrative: what the conquerors' book left out.
