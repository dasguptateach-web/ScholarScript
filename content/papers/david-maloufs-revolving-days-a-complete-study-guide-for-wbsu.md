David Malouf's "Revolving Days": A Complete Study Guide for WBSU English Honours Semester 7  
Introduction  
David Malouf (born 1934) is one of Australia's most celebrated contemporary writers—a poet, novelist, essayist, and librettist who received the Neustadt International Prize for Literature in 2000. "Revolving Days" was first published in the anthology Typewriter Music (2007) and later became the title poem of his selected poems collection, Revolving Days (2008). The poem is a contemplative lyric about memory, love, and the persistence of the past in the present. As Malouf himself stated in his author's note, the collection is "about poetry and its relationship with time and memory".  

### Complete Text of the Poem
### Revolving Days
--David Malouf  

That year I had nowhere to go, I fell in love —  
a mistake of course, but it lasted  
and has lasted. The old tug  
at the heart, the grace unasked for,  
urgencies that boom under the pocket of a shirt.  

What I remember is the colour  
of the shirts. I'd bought them  
as an experiment in ways of seeing  
myself, hoping to catch in a window  
as I passed what I was to be  
in my new life as lover: one mint  
green, one pink, the third, called Ivy  
League, tan with darker stripes, my first  
button-down collar.  

We never write. But sometimes,  
knotting my tie at a mirror, one  
of those selves I had expected  
steps into the room. In the next room  
you are waiting (we have not yet  
taken back the life we promised  
to pour into each other's mouths  
forever and for ever) while I choose  
between changes to surprise you. Revolving days.  

My heart in my mouth again,  
I'm writing this for you, wherever you  
are, whoever is staring into your blue eyes.  
It is me, I'm still here. No, don't worry,  
I won't appear out of that old time  
to discomfort you. And no, at this distance,  
I'm not holding my breath for a reply.

### Stanza-wise Summary
First Stanza (Lines 1–5)  
The speaker begins by recalling a particular year when he "had nowhere to go" and "fell in love." He immediately qualifies this by calling it "a mistake of course," yet the repetition of "it lasted and has lasted" reveals that the emotional consequences have been enduring. The "old tug at the heart" and the "grace unasked for" suggest a mixture of nostalgia and involuntary emotional response. The "urgencies that boom under the pocket of a shirt" is a striking image that connects physical garments with the powerful, almost audible pulse of desire and longing. The shirt becomes a symbol of the speaker's emotional state—something worn close to the body, concealing yet pulsing with feeling.  

Second Stanza (Lines 6–14)

The speaker shifts to a specific memory: the colors of the shirts he bought. He describes them as "an experiment in ways of seeing myself"—that is, an attempt to construct a new identity as a lover. He hoped to catch glimpses of this new self in shop windows as he passed. The shirts—"one mint green, one pink, the third, called Ivy League, tan with darker stripes"—are vividly particular. The "first button-down collar" signifies a rite of passage into adult sophistication. These details are not incidental; they reveal how the speaker associated his transformation as a lover with material self-fashioning. The colors become symbols of possibility and the hopes invested in the relationship.

Third Stanza (Lines 15–23)

The speaker acknowledges the present reality: "We never write." The relationship has ended, yet memory continues to intrude. Sometimes, while knotting his tie at a mirror, one of those old selves—the selves he had expected to become—"steps into the room." The present and past collapse into each other. He imagines the lover waiting "in the next room," and for a moment, the old promise—"to pour into each other's mouths / forever and for ever"—remains unbroken. The phrase "Revolving days" captures the cyclical nature of memory: days and selves endlessly circling back.

Fourth Stanza (Lines 24–30)

The final stanza returns to the present with the speaker declaring, "My heart in my mouth again." He is writing to the lost lover "wherever" she is, whoever is now with her. The address is tender yet resigned. "It is me, I'm still here" asserts continuity of self despite the passage of time. The repeated "No" and "And no" signal a gentle but firm reassurance: he will not intrude, he will not "appear out of that old time / to discomfort" her. The closing line—"I'm not holding my breath for a reply"—is a poignant acceptance of the one-sided nature of this communication. It is a letter that will never be sent, a declaration that expects no answer.

Analysis

"Revolving Days" is a three-stanza poem divided into uneven sets of lines: the first stanza contains ten lines, the second seven, and the third six. Malouf does not employ a regular rhyme scheme or metrical pattern; instead, the lines vary in syllabic length, creating a conversational, meditative rhythm. This free-verse structure mirrors the poem's central concern: the unpredictable, non-linear movement of memory.

The title itself is deeply suggestive. "Revolving" implies cyclical motion—days that keep returning, identities that keep circling. A revolving door allows passage but also brings you back to where you started. The poem suggests that for the speaker, the past is never entirely past. The "days" are not merely passing; they are turning, returning, demanding attention.

The poem's temporal structure is crucial. It moves between past ("That year"), present ("I'm writing this"), and a hypothetical suspended time (the imagined "next room" where the lover still waits). This oscillation between temporal registers is what makes the poem so effective as a meditation on memory. The speaker is simultaneously in multiple times—a phenomenon that Malouf, as a writer deeply preoccupied with "the startlingly achronological properties of memory," explores throughout his work.

The act of writing is also central. The speaker is "writing this for you," transforming memory into text. The poem is itself a letter that will never be sent, an address to an absent person. This makes it an instance of apostrophe—a poetic address to someone who cannot respond. As the eNotes analysis notes, the poem "uses apostrophe and symbolism to convey the difficulty of moving on".

### Poetic Devices
1. Alliteration: Malouf employs alliteration in phrases such as "life as lover" (line 8) and "writing" and "wherever" in the final stanza. The repetition of initial consonant sounds creates a musical, incantatory quality.

2. Anaphora: The repetition of "No" and "And no" in the final stanza creates emphasis and emotional weight. It is a rhetorical device that underscores the speaker's insistence and reassurance.

3. Caesura: The poem frequently splits lines with punctuation, creating deliberate pauses. For instance, "Revolving days. My heart" creates a moment of stillness before the emotional register intensifies.

4. Enjambment: Lines flow into one another without pause, mimicking the continuous movement of thought and memory. This is particularly notable in the first stanza, where "a mistake of course, but it lasted / and has lasted" carries the reader across the line break.

5. Symbolism: The colored shirts symbolize the speaker's hoped-for new identity. The "mint green," "pink," and "Ivy League" tan shirts represent different possibilities, different selves he tried on. As one analysis observes, "The colorful brightness of those shirts . . . are the only colors in the poem. Life seems as though it is, perhaps, figuratively colorless now for him".

6. Apostrophe: The entire poem is addressed to an absent lover—"wherever you are, whoever is staring into your blue eyes." This device creates intimacy while simultaneously emphasizing separation.

7. Half-rhyme/Assonance: Malouf uses partial rhymes such as "green" and "League" and "seeing" and "be" to create subtle sonic connections.

Themes

## 1. Love and Its Persistence

The central theme is the enduring nature of love, even after the relationship has ended. The speaker calls falling in love "a mistake," yet the emotion "has lasted." The poem explores the paradox of a love that is both a source of pain and a defining feature of the self.

## 2. Memory and Time

The poem is fundamentally about the relationship between memory and time. The "revolving days" of the title suggest that time is not linear but cyclical. Memory brings the past into the present, collapsing temporal distance. The speaker's present self coexists with the selves he once hoped to become.

## 3. Identity and Self-Fashioning

The shirts represent the speaker's attempt to construct a new identity as a lover. The act of buying clothes is presented as "an experiment in ways of seeing myself." This theme connects to Malouf's broader preoccupation with "the relationship between innocence and experience".

## 4. Loss and Resignation

The final stanza is marked by resignation. The speaker does not expect a reply; he does not intend to intrude. This is not bitterness but a mature acceptance of separation. The tone is one of "resigned and contemplative" reflection.

## 5. The Act of Writing as Memorial

The poem itself is an act of memorial. By writing, the speaker preserves the memory of the relationship and the person he loved. The letter that will never be sent becomes a monument to what was.

### Critics' Opinions

Sarah Holland-Batt, reviewing Revolving Days for The Sydney Morning Herald, called it a "masterwork of life and land," praising Malouf's lyrical exploration of landscape and memory.

Bev Braune, writing for Cordite Poetry Review, noted that in Revolving Days, Malouf "addresses the past, place and its importance to self-definition, the memory of houses emptied of family and objects yet full of what's left behind and filling up the present". Braune also observed that Malouf's work exhibits "political comments more subtle than Les Murray's and longings less romanticised than Robert Adamson's".

The eGyanKosh unit on Malouf suggests that "Revolving Days" is a "suitable poem where the title absolutely fits in as, not only for the strength of its address to a former love, but for its reference of the different selves in one's life".

Conclusion

"Revolving Days" is a deceptively simple poem that rewards close reading. Its conversational tone, vivid imagery, and emotional honesty make it accessible, while its complex temporal structure and symbolic depth make it worthy of sustained critical attention. For WBSU English Honours students, the poem offers rich material for exploring themes of memory, identity, love, and loss within a postcolonial Australian context. Malouf's achievement lies in his ability to render the most intimate, personal experience in language that feels both particular and universal.
