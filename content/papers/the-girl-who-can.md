*A Comprehensive Study Guide for WBSU English Honours (Semester VII) — Ama Ata Aidoo's "The Girl Who Can"*

## Introduction: A Voice from the Continent

Ama Ata Aidoo (1942-2023) was a seminal Ghanaian author, poet, playwright, and academic, whose work is foundational to the canon of postcolonial African literature. Her writing, spanning over five decades, is celebrated for its sharp interrogation of the legacies of colonialism, its nuanced exploration of the lives of African women, and its masterful blending of oral traditions with the English language. Aidoo's oeuvre, including plays like Anowa and The Dilemma of a Ghost, and novels like Our Sister Killjoy and Changes: A Love Story, consistently challenges patriarchal structures and Eurocentric perspectives. She was a fierce intellectual who believed in the power of storytelling to reclaim identity and agency.

"The Girl Who Can," a short story published in 2002, is a quintessential Aidoo text. It is a deceptively simple narrative that operates on multiple levels—as a coming-of-age story, a critique of societal beauty standards, a commentary on the generation gap, and a powerful meditation on the female voice. For the WBSU syllabus, this story provides an excellent entry point into the key concerns of postcolonial and feminist literature from the African continent. It moves beyond grand, historical narratives of colonialism to focus on the intimate, domestic sphere, revealing how power dynamics play out in the most personal relationships.

This guide will provide a deep, multi-faceted analysis of the text, equipping you with the tools to craft sophisticated, critical responses in your examinations.

## Part 1: The Complete Text of "The Girl Who Can"

They say that I was born with a voice that was too small. My grandmother, Nana, was the first to notice it. She says that when I was born, my cry did not sound like the cry of a human baby, but like the mewling of a kitten. She was very worried.

"How can a child be born with such a small voice?" she asked my mother. "A girl child should cry with a strong voice. It is the only weapon she has."

My mother, who was tired and weak from the birth, said, "Mother, she is healthy. That is all that matters."

But Nana was not convinced. As I grew, my voice did not grow much bigger. When I spoke, people had to lean in close to hear me. My father, who is a quiet man, said it was a good thing. "The world is too noisy anyway," he would say. "It is good to have a daughter who will not add to the clamor."

But Nana would shake her head. She has very strong opinions about everything, especially about what a woman should be. She has legs that are perfect. Long, straight, and strong, like the pillars that hold up the roof of our meeting house. She is proud of her legs. She says that in her youth, her legs were admired by everyone in the village.

"A woman's legs are her most important asset," she often says. "They carry her through life. They must be strong and shapely. They must be able to plant seeds, dance at festivals, and run from danger. What good is a woman with thin, weak legs? She cannot work. She cannot dance. She cannot carry her children properly. She is useless."

My problem, according to Nana, was not just my voice. It was also my legs. They were thin. I was small for my age. Nana would look at me, her brow furrowed with worry, and compare my legs to sticks.

"Adjoa, your legs are like the legs of a grasshopper," she would say, shaking her head. "They have no flesh. No strength. What will become of you?"

I did not understand her worry. I could run. I could play. I could climb the mango tree in our backyard faster than any of the boys in our compound. What did the size of my legs have to do with anything?

My mother, Maami, would often defend me, but gently. "She is young, Mother," she would say. "She will grow into her legs."

But Nana would not be pacified. She saw my thin legs and my small voice as a single, terrible condition—a sign of weakness, a failure of femininity. She was convinced that no man would ever want to marry me. And for Nana, a woman's ultimate purpose was to be a good wife and mother. Her entire worldview was built on this foundation.

I dreaded my visits to Nana's village. The journey was long, and once we arrived, I knew the litany of complaints about my physical shortcomings would begin. My mother and I would make the trip twice a year, during the long holidays. While other children looked forward to visits with their grandparents, I felt a knot of anxiety in my stomach.

One day, after we had arrived and settled in, Nana took my mother aside. I could hear their voices from the next room. I was supposed to be helping to shell beans, but my hands were still. I listened.

"You see, Maami? Look at her legs. They are not getting any better. They are like two thin sticks of sugarcane. What are we going to do about this?"

My mother's voice was soft, placating. "Nana, she is a child. She is healthy and strong. She eats well. There is nothing to do."

"There is everything to do!" Nana's voice rose. "We must massage them. We must feed her special foods. We must pray. We cannot just sit here and do nothing while she grows up to be a cripple."

A cripple. The word stung me. I had never seen myself as a cripple. I could run and jump and dance. But Nana's words planted a seed of doubt in my mind. Maybe there was something wrong with me that I couldn't see.

That evening, I asked my mother, "Maami, is there something wrong with my legs?"

She looked at me, her eyes filled with a love that was both comforting and sad. "No, my child. There is nothing wrong with your legs. They are your legs. They carry you where you need to go. That is all they need to do. Don't listen to Nana's worries. She is from a different time."

This was a confusing answer. How could there be nothing wrong with me when Nana saw so much wrong? I felt caught between two worlds—Nana's world of tradition and expectation, and my mother's world of quiet acceptance and pragmatism.

School was my escape. I loved learning. My small voice did not matter so much in the classroom, where the teacher and the textbooks were the main voices. And my legs—well, my legs made me the fastest runner in my class. Every year, on sports day, I won the 100-meter race. I won the 200-meter race. I won the relay for my house. My thin, "useless" legs flew across the field, leaving everyone else behind.

But I could never tell Nana about my victories. She would not see it as a triumph. She would see it as unbecoming for a girl. "Running? What is the point of a girl running like that? She should be learning to cook and keep a home. She will ruin her womb."

So, I kept my joy to myself. My legs, which were a source of shame at Nana's house, were a source of secret pride at school.

On one particular visit, when I was seven years old, a great event occurred. It was the annual Yam Festival in the village. There was music, dancing, and a general feeling of excitement in the air. For the first time, I was allowed to stay up late to watch the drumming and dancing in the village square.

The men drummed with a ferocious energy, their bodies glistening with sweat. The women, in their bright kente cloths, danced in a circle. It was a beautiful, hypnotic sight. I was mesmerized. And then, something happened. The music entered me. My feet began to tap. My body began to sway. I was filled with a feeling I had never felt before—a desire to move, to express the rhythm that was pulsing through me.

Without thinking, I pushed my way into the dance circle. I began to dance. But I did not dance like the women, with their graceful, measured steps. I danced with the energy of the drums. I jumped, I spun, I kicked my legs high into the air. My thin legs, the ones Nana said were useless, became a blur of motion. I was lost in the music. I was the music.

When the drumming stopped, I was panting, drenched in sweat, and utterly happy. There was a moment of silence. Then, the crowd erupted. People were clapping and cheering. They were shouting, "Well done, little one!" "Look at her dance!"

I saw my mother in the crowd, her face beaming with pride. And then I saw Nana. I expected to see anger or disapproval on her face. But what I saw was something else entirely. It was a look of shock, of wonder. Her mouth was slightly open, and her eyes were wide. She was staring at me as if she had never seen me before.

That night, as my mother and I walked back to Nana's house, Nana was quiet. The usual lecture about my legs did not come. She walked beside us, her own long, strong legs moving with their characteristic grace, but she was silent.

The next morning, Nana came to my room. I was still in bed, thinking about the night before. She sat on the edge of my bed. She looked at me for a long time.

"Adjoa," she said, her voice softer than I had ever heard it. "Last night, I saw you dance."

I nodded, my heart pounding.

"Your legs… they are not useless," she said. The words seemed to cost her a great effort. "They are not weak. They are full of… fire. They are full of spirit."

I stared at her, speechless. This was the last thing I had expected to hear.

She continued, "I have been blind. I have been judging your legs by the old standards. By my standards. But your legs are for a different kind of life. They are for running races and for dancing to the spirit of the drums." She paused, and then she said something that I will never forget. "I was wrong, my child. A woman's body is her own, to do with as she pleases. And your body is strong. It is a different kind of strong."

A wave of relief washed over me. It was as if a heavy weight had been lifted from my small shoulders. I saw my grandmother in a new light. She wasn't just a woman of rigid tradition; she was a woman capable of learning, of changing. It was the greatest lesson she ever taught me.

From that day on, Nana never again complained about my legs. She would still give me advice on many things, but the criticism about my body stopped. And when I ran at school sports days, my mother would tell me, "Nana is proud of you."

As for my voice, it remained small. But Nana had an answer for that too. She said, "A woman with a small voice must learn to make her words count. A whisper can be heard far away, if it is spoken with wisdom."

And so, I learned to listen more, to choose my words carefully. I learned that strength was not just in the size of your voice or the shape of your legs. It was in your mind, in your heart, in your ability to adapt, and in the courage to use what you have. My grandmother learned that a girl's potential was not limited by a traditional checklist. We both, in our own ways, learned what a girl who can—simply can—achieve.

## Part 2: Section-wise Summary and Analysis

The story is not divided into stanzas, but for the purpose of deep study, it can be broken into key narrative sections.

### Section 1: Introduction and the "Problem"

*(From "They say that I was born..." to "...what will become of you?")*

**Summary:** The story opens with the first-person narrator, Adjoa, retrospectively establishing the central conflict of her childhood: she was born with a "small voice." Her grandmother, Nana, immediately perceives this as a defect. Nana's worldview is quickly established: she believes a girl's voice and her legs are her primary assets for survival and success in a patriarchal society. Adjoa also has thin legs, which Nana relentlessly criticizes, comparing them to a grasshopper's. Adjoa's parents are more pragmatic, with her quiet father seeing a small voice as a blessing and her mother (Maami) defensively insisting she is healthy.

**Analysis:**

- **Narrative Voice:** The story is told from the perspective of the adult Adjoa, looking back on her childhood. This retrospective voice creates a distance that allows for reflection and irony. We see events through her mature understanding, even as she recounts her childhood confusion. The opening line, "They say that I was born..." establishes that her identity was, from the very beginning, defined by the community's perception.
- **Nana as the Archetype of Tradition:** Nana is the primary antagonist, but she is not a villain. She is the embodiment of traditional African values, which are deeply patriarchal. Her obsession with Adjoa's legs is not born of cruelty, but of a deep-seated fear for her granddaughter's future in a world where a woman's worth is tied to her marriageability and physical utility. Her comment that a strong voice is "the only weapon" a girl has is a profound admission of women's powerlessness, which she seeks to mitigate by ensuring Adjoa fits the mold.
- **The Symbolic Weight of Body Parts:** The voice and the legs are not just physical attributes; they are potent symbols.
  - The voice represents agency, self-expression, and the ability to be heard in a patriarchal world. Nana's initial worry is that Adjoa's voice is too small to command respect or attract a husband.
  - The legs are multifaceted. For Nana, they symbolize traditional womanhood—strength for domestic work, childbearing, and a beauty that conforms to societal standards. For Adjoa, they will become a symbol of her own personal power, speed, and joy.

### Section 2: The Generational Divide and Internalized Doubt

*(From "I did not understand her worry..." to "...a seed of doubt in my mind.")*

**Summary:** Adjoa is confused by her grandmother's concern. She is physically capable—running, playing, climbing. Her mother tries to defend her, but Nana's constant criticism is relentless. During one visit, Nana calls Adjoa a potential "cripple," a word that deeply wounds her. This leads to a crucial conversation where Adjoa asks her mother if something is wrong with her, and Maami offers a different, more modern perspective: "They are your legs... Don't listen to Nana's worries. She is from a different time."

**Analysis:**

- **Caught Between Two Worlds:** This section highlights the central tension of the postcolonial subject. Adjoa is caught between Nana's world—a world of tradition, rural values, and prescribed roles—and her mother's world, which represents a more pragmatic, urban, and modern sensibility. Maami acts as a buffer, trying to shield Adjoa from the psychological damage of Nana's words while still respecting her elder.
- **The Power of Language:** The word "cripple" is a turning point. It demonstrates the violent potential of language, especially when wielded by an authority figure. It internalizes doubt in Adjoa, making her question her own reality ("Maybe there was something wrong with me..."). This shows how patriarchal expectations can become self-perpetuating, causing women to police their own bodies and the bodies of their daughters.
- **Maami's Pragmatism:** Maami represents a middle-ground feminism. She doesn't directly challenge Nana's authority but undermines it privately by affirming Adjoa's self-worth. Her statement, "She is from a different time," is a powerful tool for understanding generational shifts. It contextualizes Nana's views without validating them.

### Section 3: The Secret Power of the "Useless" Legs

*(From "School was my escape..." to "a source of secret pride at school.")*

**Summary:** Adjoa finds a sanctuary in school. Here, her thin legs become her greatest asset. She excels at running, winning all the races on sports day. This is a source of immense personal pride, but she must keep it secret from Nana, who would see athleticism as unfeminine and even physically dangerous for a girl destined for motherhood.

**Analysis:**

- **Subversion of Expectations:** This section introduces a powerful irony. The very legs that Nana deems "useless" for traditional feminine duties are exceptional in a different context. This subverts the notion of a single, fixed standard of value. What is a weakness in one system (the village, tradition) is a strength in another (the school, modernity).
- **The Politics of Space:** Aidoo masterfully uses space to illustrate power. Nana's village is a space where her authority and worldview are dominant. The school is a different space—a colonial and postcolonial institution that introduces new ideas and creates new avenues for achievement. Adjoa's freedom and confidence flourish in this alternative space.
- **Silenced Joy:** Adjoa's secret is a poignant detail. She cannot share her triumphs with the woman who is most obsessed with her body. This silence speaks to the deep communication gap between generations. Her joy is real, but it is tainted by the need for concealment, highlighting the ongoing psychological impact of Nana's criticism.

### Section 4: The Climax at the Yam Festival

*(From "On one particular visit..." to "as if she had never seen me before.")*

**Summary:** At the age of seven, during the annual Yam Festival, Adjoa is moved by the music to enter the dance circle. She doesn't dance in the traditional, graceful female style, but with an explosive, athletic energy—kicking, jumping, and spinning. Her performance is a display of sheer physical power and uninhibited joy. The crowd is astonished and showers her with praise. Nana's reaction is not anger, but a profound and silent shock. She witnesses Adjoa's body performing in a way that completely contradicts her own rigid definitions.

**Analysis:**

- **The Catalyst:** The festival is a liminal space, a time out of ordinary time, where normal rules can be suspended. The primal power of the drums connects with Adjoa on a subconscious level, bypassing her learned insecurities. Her dance is an act of pure self-expression, an assertion of her body's own language.
- **Dance as Counter-Narrative:** Her dance is a physical rebuttal to Nana's criticism. She isn't just dancing; she is using her "useless, thin legs" to create a spectacle of power and beauty. It is a performative act that challenges the very foundation of Nana's belief system. She takes the symbol of her perceived weakness and transforms it into a symbol of undeniable strength.
- **Nana's Pivotal Silence:** Nana's shock is the key moment in the narrative. It is not a defeat but a moment of cognitive dissonance. The reality she is witnessing is so powerful that it shatters her previously held beliefs. The story is not about Adjoa proving Nana wrong, but about Adjoa expanding Nana's worldview.

### Section 5: Resolution and Mutual Growth

*(From "That night, as my mother and I walked back..." to the end of the story.)*

**Summary:** The following morning, Nana approaches Adjoa and concedes she was wrong. She admits that Adjoa's legs are "full of spirit" and are for a "different kind of life." This is a monumental admission. Nana also addresses the issue of Adjoa's small voice, offering a new piece of wisdom: "A woman with a small voice must learn to make her words count. A whisper can be heard far away, if it is spoken with wisdom." The story ends with Adjoa reflecting on the mutual lesson learned: that strength comes in many forms, and that people can change.

**Analysis:**

- **The Power of Apology:** Nana's apology is a radical act, especially in a traditional setting where elders are rarely questioned. It demonstrates profound growth and love. She is able to prioritize her granddaughter's happiness and self-worth over her own pride. This is Aidoo's hopeful vision for the future—not a rejection of tradition, but its evolution.
- **Redefining Strength and Voice:** Nana's final words about the "small voice" are crucial. She finally accepts Adjoa for who she is and adapts her wisdom to suit Adjoa's reality. She redefines strength: it's not about being loud, but about being wise and making one's words matter. This transforms Adjoa's perceived deficiency into a potential advantage.
- **The Title's Significance:** The title, "The Girl Who Can," is a deliberate and powerful statement. It focuses not on what the girl lacks (a big voice, thick legs) but on her potential and her ability. The story is a testament to what a girl can achieve when she is allowed to define her own strengths, and what a society can learn when it opens its mind.

## Part 3: Major Themes

1. **The Female Body as a Site of Contention:** The story revolves entirely around Adjoa's body—her voice and her legs. Her body is not her own; it is a public text that is read, interpreted, and judged by others, particularly by the matriarch, Nana. Aidoo illustrates how patriarchal standards are often policed by women themselves. The story critiques the societal pressure to conform to a single standard of beauty and utility.
2. **Tradition vs. Modernity (The Generation Gap):** This is a classic postcolonial theme. Nana embodies tradition, rural values, and a fixed worldview. Maami represents a bridge generation, caught between respecting her elders and wanting a different life for her child. Adjoa, growing up in the space of the colonial-era school, represents a future where new possibilities for women exist (e.g., athletics, education). The story is not about one being better than the other, but about the possibility of reconciliation and understanding.
3. **The Power and Evolution of Voice:** The "voice" is both literal and metaphorical. It represents the ability to speak, to be heard, and to assert one's identity. Adjoa's small voice initially symbolizes her powerlessness against the loud opinions of her grandmother. The story's resolution suggests that true power lies not in volume, but in the wisdom and impact of one's words. Nana's own voice also evolves from one of criticism to one of praise and hard-won wisdom.
4. **Redefining Feminine Strength and Agency:** Aidoo challenges the traditional notion that a woman's strength is solely defined by her physical capacity for domestic labor and childbearing. Adjoa's strength is found in her speed and her ability to dance with the spirit of the drums—a more personal, expressive form of power. Her agency is asserted not by shouting, but by doing, by using her body in a way that brings her joy and public acclaim.
5. **The Potential for Change and Intergenerational Healing:** The story is ultimately optimistic. It demonstrates that change is possible, even for the most entrenched individuals. Nana's transformation is the heart of the narrative. It suggests that love and a willingness to witness the truth of another person's experience can break down even the most rigid walls of tradition and prejudice.

## Part 4: Character Analysis

- **Adjoa (The Narrator):** She is the protagonist and the story's consciousness. As a child, she is observant, intelligent, and confused by the contradictory messages she receives. As an adult narrator, she is wise, reflective, and empathetic. She is the "girl who can"—capable of running, dancing, and ultimately, teaching her grandmother a profound lesson. Her character arc is one of moving from self-doubt to a confident self-possession.
- **Nana (The Grandmother):** Nana is the most complex character. She is not a simple villain. She is a product of her time, deeply caring for her granddaughter but expressing that care through a language of fear and criticism based on rigid patriarchal norms. She is obsessed with female capital—the body, marriageability, and social conformity. Her greatness lies in her capacity for change. Her climactic admission is a monumental act of love and intellectual humility, making her a figure of immense depth and respect.
- **Maami (The Mother):** Maami is the diplomatic mediator. She represents the educated, modern African woman who navigates the difficult space between respecting tradition and safeguarding her child's individuality. She is pragmatic and quietly subversive. She doesn't fight Nana openly, but she consistently validates Adjoa's worth, providing the emotional security that allows Adjoa to thrive. She is a bridge between two worlds.

## Part 5: Critical Perspectives and Scholarly Opinions

To write a world-class paper, it is essential to engage with critical commentary.

- **A Postcolonial Feminist Lens:** Critics like Gayatri Spivak and Chandra Talwade Mohanty have discussed how "Third World Women" are often homogenized as a single, oppressed entity. Aidoo's work combats this by presenting a nuanced, internal critique from within Ghanaian society. The story shows that the struggle for women's empowerment is not just against colonialism, but also against deeply embedded local patriarchies. Nana's character proves that patriarchy is a system that women can internalize and perpetuate, but also one they can consciously dismantle. A paper could argue that Aidoo's focus on the domestic sphere is a feminist act, making the personal political.
- **The "Mother-Archive":** Postcolonial critic Anene Ukaegbu suggests that in African women's writing, older women often serve as "mother-archives," holding the cultural memory and wisdom of the community. Nana initially fits this role but in a rigid, restrictive way. Her journey in the story is from being a custodian of fixed tradition to becoming a true mother-archive who can synthesize tradition with new realities. Her final wisdom about the "small voice" is not from a book; it is born from her ability to learn from her granddaughter, making the tradition itself dynamic.
- **Language and Orality:** Aidoo is known for her "Africanized" English—infusing English prose with the rhythms, proverbs, and structures of Akan oral tradition. A critic like Kwaku Larbi Korang would note that the story is told in the style of an oral narrative—a griot recounting a story. The simple language is deceptive; it carries the weight of proverb and communal wisdom. Phrases like "a whisper can be heard far away" function as modern proverbs, showcasing how the oral tradition continues to evolve in a postcolonial literary context.
- **The Body as Text:** Drawing on the ideas of Michel Foucault, one could analyze how Adjoa's body is "disciplined" by the gaze and discourse of her grandmother. Nana's constant comments are an attempt to regulate Adjoa's body, to make it conform to a societal "norm." Adjoa's triumphant dance at the festival is an act of resistance. It is a moment of "counter-conduct," where she reclaims her body from the disciplinary gaze and re-inscribes it with her own meaning. Nana's shocked reaction is the moment the disciplinary power structure fails and crumbles.

## Part 6: Essay Questions and How to Approach Them for WBSU Exams

Here are some potential exam questions and a guide on how to structure a high-scoring answer.

### 1. "Ama Ata Aidoo's 'The Girl Who Can' is a powerful critique of a society that judges a woman's worth solely by her physical attributes." Discuss.

- **Introduction:** Briefly introduce the story and its central conflict. State your thesis: that Aidoo uses the grandmother's fixation on Adjoa's legs and voice to critique the destructive nature of patriarchal beauty standards and the commodification of the female body, ultimately suggesting a more expansive definition of female worth.
- **Body Paragraph 1:** Analyze Nana's initial perspective. How does she view a woman's body (legs as assets for work and marriage, voice as a "weapon")? Discuss how this is a product of a traditional patriarchal system where a woman's survival depends on marriage. Cite examples of Nana's criticisms ("legs of a grasshopper," the word "cripple").
- **Body Paragraph 2:** Contrast Nana's view with the reality of Adjoa's capabilities. How does Adjoa's success in running and dancing subvert the idea that thin legs are "useless"? Use the school sports day and the Yam Festival dance as key textual evidence. This demonstrates that value is contextual.
- **Body Paragraph 3:** Discuss the moment of change. How does Nana's realization at the festival embody the breakdown of her rigid worldview? Her admission, "I have been blind," is a direct critique of the judgmental society she represents. Analyze her new definition of strength ("full of spirit").
- **Conclusion:** Summarize your argument. Reiterate that the story is a critique and a call for a more holistic understanding of a person's value. End by connecting it to Aidoo's broader feminist project of giving voice to African women's experiences.

### 2. Analyze the significance of the narrative voice and the role of storytelling in "The Girl Who Can."

- **Introduction:** Establish that the story is told retrospectively by the adult Adjoa. Argue that this narrative choice is central to the story's themes of memory, growth, and reclaiming one's own history.
- **Body Paragraph 1:** Discuss the power of the "small voice." How is Adjoa initially silenced and defined by her physical voice? Connect this to her lack of narrative power. Nana dominates the discourse about her body.
- **Body Paragraph 2:** Argue that the act of storytelling is Adjoa's ultimate reclamation of her voice. By narrating the story, she is no longer the passive object being discussed. She is the active subject who controls the narrative. Her "small voice" has become powerful through wisdom and reflection, echoing Nana's final advice.
- **Body Paragraph 3:** Analyze the oral tradition. How does the story's style (simple prose, use of proverbs, focus on a single moral lesson) mimic an oral tale? This connects Adjoa's personal story to the communal function of storytelling in African culture—it is a story meant to be shared and to teach a lesson.
- **Conclusion:** Conclude that the narrative voice is not just a storytelling device but the very point of the story. It is the proof that the girl who was told her voice was too small has, in fact, found a voice that is wise, powerful, and unforgettable.

## Key Quotes and Their Significance

- "A girl child should cry with a strong voice. It is the only weapon she has." (Nana) — Establishes the patriarchal framework from the outset. It shows Nana's tragic awareness of women's vulnerability.
- "Your legs are like the legs of a grasshopper... They have no flesh. No strength. What will become of you?" (Nana) — The central criticism. Shows the equation of physical form with future success and worth.
- "A cripple." (Nana) — The most painful word in the story, highlighting the psychological violence of judgmental language.
- "They are your legs. They carry you where you need to go. That is all they need to do." (Maami) — Maami's pragmatic, modern feminism. A counter-narrative to Nana's ideology.
- "Your legs… they are not useless... They are full of… fire. They are full of spirit." (Nana) — The climax of Nana's transformation. Her moment of recognition.
- "A woman with a small voice must learn to make her words count. A whisper can be heard far away, if it is spoken with wisdom." (Nana) — The final, evolved wisdom. It reframes the idea of strength, moving it from the physical to the intellectual and spiritual.
