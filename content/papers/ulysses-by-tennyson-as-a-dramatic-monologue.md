### Introduction: The Dramatic Monologue and Victorian Anxiety

Alfred Lord Tennyson's "Ulysses" (1842) stands as the prototypical Victorian dramatic monologue, a form that Robert Langbaum defines as a poem where "the speaker is dramatised, and the reader is placed in the position of the auditor". Written in the immediate aftermath of Arthur Henry Hallam's death, the poem transcends mere elegy to become what Tennyson himself described as giving "his feeling about Hallam's death... the need for going forward, and braving the struggle of life". Yet the dramatic monologue form complicates any straightforward reading, creating an ironic gap between the speaker's self-presentation and the reader's understanding—a tension that has generated diverse critical responses for over a century.

### Dramatic Features: Voice, Auditor, and Structure

The poem's dramatic character emerges immediately through its structure as a speech delivered in a specific situation. Ulysses stands on the "still hearth" among "barren crags," addressing an implied audience that remains unnamed until line 45: "My mariners, / Souls that have toil'd, and wrought, and thought with me". This delayed revelation creates what critics identify as a "mild shock"—a hallmark of the dramatic monologue's ability to reveal character through situational disclosure. The blank verse paragraphs mimic the unrhymed iambic pentameter of Shakespearean soliloquy, yet Tennyson uses the form to craft a speaker whose shifting moods and rhetorical strategies expose his psychological complexity.

**The Ironic Perspective:** Heroic Idealist or Escapist King?

The poem's dramatic irony operates through the gap between Ulysses' heroic self-fashioning and the domestic reality he describes with undisguised contempt. He speaks of "an idle king, / By this still hearth, among these barren crags, / Match'd with an aged wife"—words that critics like Glennis Byron argue reveal "snarling impatience" rather than noble discontent. The famous passage "I cannot rest from travel: I will drink / Life to the lees" (lines 6-7) can be read as either heroic refusal of stagnation or as a "selfish" desire to "abandon his kingdom and family". This irony is intensified by the poem's unspoken context: the Trojan War's destruction, referenced obliquely in "the deep / Moans round with many voices" (lines 55-56), suggests that Ulysses' celebrated heroism is inseparable from violence.

### Critical Controversies: Victorian Heroism vs. Modern Irony

Victorian readers largely received the poem as an affirmation of "the necessity of striving ever onward," as George Landow notes, supported by Tennyson's own comments about Hallam. However, modern critics have foregrounded the poem's ambiguity. Langbaum observes that the "same weariness and longing for rest" that characterizes "The Lotos-Eaters" is the "emotional bias of Tennyson's finest dramatic monologue, Ulysses; though here the emotion is couched in the contrasting language of adventure". He cautions against reading the poem "in the manner of Browning," arguing that such a reading uses "the head only and not the sensibility". This tension between surface celebration and submerged despair defines the poem's dramatic power.

### Language and Imagery: The Fading Margin

Tennyson's imagery reinforces the monologue's dramatic tension. The metaphor "all experience is an arch wherethro' / Gleams that untravell'd world whose margin fades / For ever and for ever when I move" (lines 19-21) captures both the promise and the futility of endless questing. Critics note that the fading "margin" projects "future adventure" while simultaneously imaging "Ulysses's distance from the heroism of his past". Similarly, "To follow knowledge like a sinking star, / Beyond the utmost bound of human thought" (lines 31-32) renders aspiration as decline—a star sinking rather than rising. The aged hero acknowledges his diminished capacity: "We are not now that strength which in old days / Moved earth and heaven" (lines 66-67), yet resolves "To strive, to seek, to find, and not to yield" (line 70)—words that sound heroic but may mask a death-wish.

### Conclusion: The Dramatic Monologue as Psychological Portraiture

"Ulysses" exemplifies the dramatic monologue's capacity to present character as process rather than essence. The poem resists resolution because its speaker does: he is neither purely heroic nor simply deluded, but a complex figure whose rhetoric simultaneously reveals and conceals his motives. As the Britannica entry notes, "the ironic distance of the narrative voice intensifies the ambiguity". Tennyson's achievement lies in creating a voice so compelling that readers—like Ulysses' mariners—are drawn into his vision even as they question its wisdom. The poem remains exam-worthy precisely because it demands that we hold heroic sympathy and critical skepticism in productive tension.
