Few poems bridge the gap between a short story and its aftermath with the haunting intimacy of Gulzar’s Toba Tek Singh. Written as a lyrical sequel to Saadat Hasan Manto’s immortal 1955 story, this poem does not merely retell; it reopens the wound. Gulzar, displaced by Partition as a child, refuses to let Manto’s madman rest. Instead, he visits Bishan Singh at Wagah, not to bid farewell, but to deliver a devastating update: the madness of 1947 was only the first symptom.  
________________________________________  
### Complete Text
I’ve to go and meet Toba Tek Singh’s Bishan at Wagah  
I’m told he still stands on his swollen feet  
Where Manto had left him,  
He still mutters:  
Opad di gud gud di moong di dal di laltain  
I’ve to locate that mad fellow  
Who used to speak up from a branch high above:  
“He’s god  
He alone has to decide – whose village to whose side.”  
When will he move down that branch  
He is to be told:  
“There are some more – left still  
Who are being divided, made into pieces -  
There are some more Partitions to be done  
That Partition was only the first one.”  
I’ve to go and meet Toba Tek Singh’s Bishan at Wagah,  
His friend Afzal has to be informed -  
### Lahna Singh, Wadhwa Singh, Bheen Amrit
Had arrived here butchered -  
Their heads were looted with the luggage on the way behind.  
Slay that “Bhuri”, none will come to claim her now.  
That girl who grew one finger every twelve months,  
Now shortens one phalanx each year.  
It’s to be told that all the mad ones haven’t yet reached their destinations  
There are many on that side  
And many on this.  
Toba Tek Singh’s Bishan beckons me often to say:  
“Opad di gud gud di moong di dal di laltain di Hindustan te Pakistan di dur fitey munh.”

### Key Points for Study & Analysis
## 1. The Burden of Sequel: Why Gulzar Visits Wagah
Gulzar does not write a eulogy; he writes a continuation of trauma. Manto left Bishan Singh standing in no man’s land—a liminal figure suspended between two nations that both reject him. Gulzar’s speaker feels compelled to return because the story is unfinished. The “swollen feet” are a physical memory of fifteen years of standing without sleep, but they are also a metaphor for the unbearable weight of belonging nowhere.  
## 2. Madness as Political Critique
The “mad fellow” on the branch who claims to be God is not merely a comic figure. He is the poem’s moral compass. By declaring that only God can decide “whose village to whose side,” the madman exposes the arrogance of the Radcliffe Line—a border drawn by men who played God with millions of lives. Gulzar’s irony is sharp: the asylum inmates are labeled insane, yet it is the politicians who performed the truly irrational act of Partition.  
## 3. “That Partition Was Only the First One”
This is the poem’s most chilling line. For Gulzar, 1947 was not a singular rupture but a template for repetition. The “more Partitions to be done” are not merely territorial; they are the partitions of families, memories, and selves that continue long after the border is drawn. The poem refuses the comfort of closure.  
## 4. The Brutal Inventory: Afzal’s Grief and “Bhuri”
The roll call of the butchered—Lahna Singh, Wadhwa Singh, Bheen Amrit—transforms abstract statistics into named, decapitated bodies. Even more devastating is the image of “Bhuri,” the girl whose fingers grow and then shorten. She is the feminized body as a site of Partition violence, her gradual diminishment mirroring the slow erosion of identity and care that follows communal rupture.  
## 5. The Final Gibberish: “Dur Fitey Munh”
Bishan’s closing refrain adds “Hindustan te Pakistan di dur fitey munh”—roughly, “Curse be upon the faces of Hindustan and Pakistan.” This is not nonsense. It is the poem’s concentrated rage. The same gibberish that Manto used to signify incomprehension becomes, in Gulzar’s hands, a deliberate curse upon the nations that created the madness.  

RTC 1: “I’ve to go and meet Toba Tek Singh’s Bishan at Wagah / I’m told he still stands on his swollen feet / Where Manto had left him”

**Context:** These opening lines establish the poem’s governing conceit: Gulzar is not writing a tribute but mounting a return visit to the precise point where Manto abandoned his madman. Bishan Singh is discovered in no man’s land, physically suspended between two nations that both refuse to claim him.

**Explanation:** The phrase “swollen feet” operates simultaneously as literal pathology and political metonymy. Bishan Singh has stood for fifteen years without sleep, and the swelling is the corporeal record of that immobility. But Gulzar’s Bishan is not merely an individual sufferer: he “becomes Toba Tek Singh,” embodying the strip of land that lies, like him, between two nations. The speaker’s declaration “I’ve to go” transforms the poem into an ethical obligation, a testimony delivered to a figure who has been left waiting for news that history never delivered. The free-verse form—unrhymed, broken, without metre—formally mirrors this suspension: the lines refuse the closure of song.

**Critical Opinion:** Tuhin Sanyal argues that Gulzar “de-contextualizes Bishan Singh and his anxiety of rootlessness only to manifest him as the primordial sufferer of homelessness, who is not just a name but an experience”. This RTC is exam-essential because it rewrites Manto’s ending as a beginning: the poem’s entire argument depends on Bishan Singh still standing there, still muttering, still awaiting a report on whether the violence ever stopped.

RTC 2: “There are some more – left still / Who are being divided, made into pieces – / There are some more Partitions to be done / That Partition was only the first one.”

**Context:** These lines are the poem’s thematic hinge. The speaker imagines informing the madman on the branch—the figure who declared that only God can decide “whose village to whose side”—that the official Partition of 1947 was not a singular rupture but a template for repetition.

**Explanation:** The repetition of “more” and the verb “made into pieces” collapse the distinction between territorial cartography and bodily dismemberment. Gulzar’s original Urdu couplet is even more explicit: “abhi kuch aur bhi dil hain ke jin ko baantne ka, kaatne ka kaam zari hai / woh barwara toh pehle tha, abhi kuch aur barware baki hain”—“The task of dividing, cutting a few more hearts continues / That partition was effected earlier, some partitions are still pending”. The Shodhganga critical chapter glosses this as Gulzar “recalling the Partition as a never-ending process,” where “hearts” stand for human beings and the “earlier” Partition may even reach back to the British Divide-and-Rule policy before the Radcliffe Line was drawn.

**Critical Opinion:** Poem Analysis observes that the speaker “sees a cruel, repeating history for those caught between two new nations,” and that the poem’s irony targets the politicians who acted as though they had lost control of their minds, no different from the madman who believes himself God. This RTC should anchor any answer on Partition as process rather than event, and on the poem’s refusal of historical closure.

RTC 3: “Slay that ‘Bhuri’, none will come to claim her now. / That girl who grew one finger every twelve months, / Now shortens one phalanx each year.”

**Context:** This is the poem’s most concentrated image of gendered disposability. Bhuri is an orphaned girl whose body becomes the site where nationalist violence is enacted without witness, claim, or consequence.

**Explanation:** The imperative “Slay” implicates the reader in the killing, while “none will come to claim her” exposes the logic of communal violence as the elimination of the unclaimed. The temporal image—fingers growing, then phalanges shortening—inverts natural growth into gradual erasure: the dead body does not merely stop growing; it retracts, year by year, as if history itself is trying to unmake her. A minority critical reading notes that “Bhuri” may also echo the buffalo left behind in Manto’s story, in which case “slay” rather than “kill” carries a specifically animalised charge—the reduction of the orphan girl to livestock.

**Critical Opinion:** A widely cited study summary describes Bhuri as “mercilessly killed by nationalistic men because nobody was left to claim her,” with her shrinking body functioning as “a powerful symbol of the extreme cruelty of that time”. This RTC is indispensable for questions on women and Partition, because it moves beyond abduction-and-recovery statistics to the orphaned girl as the limit case of belonging: she is killable precisely because she belongs to no one, and no nation will retrospectively claim her corpse.

________________________________________

RTC 4: “Opad di gud gud di moong di dal di laltain di Hindustan te Pakistan di dur fitey munh.”

**Context:** The poem’s final line extends Bishan Singh’s signature gibberish with an unmistakable addition: “Hindustan te Pakistan di dur fitey munh,” rendered in critical commentary as “India and Pakistan go to the bloody hell”.

**Explanation:** In Manto, the gibberish signified the collapse of language under trauma—a sound beyond sense. Gulzar retains the nonsense but adds a target. The curse is directed at both nations equally, refusing the nationalist temptation to assign guilt to one side. The line is spoken by the madman, but its placement at the poem’s very end gives it the force of a ** verdict**. The free-verse form, which has refused rhyme and metre throughout, here refuses even the consolation of a final image: the poem ends not on a body, not on a landscape, but on a curse.

**Critical Opinion:** Poem Analysis identifies this closing addition as the poem’s moral summary: the mutterings that in Manto were merely incoherent become, in Gulzar’s hands, a deliberate indictment of the two nation-states that produced the madness. This RTC should anchor any answer on the poem’s ending as anti-closure: Bishan Singh does not find peace, the dead do not receive justice, and the subcontinent does not receive absolution. The curse is what remains when testimony has run out.
