## 1. Introduction and Background

Robert Browning’s “My Last Duchess” is one of the most famous dramatic monologues in English literature. It was first published in 1842 in Browning’s collection Dramatic Lyrics, later included in the larger collection Bells and Pomegranates. The poem is a brilliant study of power, jealousy, aristocratic pride, gender violence, and the psychology of a controlling mind. The poem is set in Renaissance Italy, in the ducal palace of Ferrara. The speaker is a Duke — probably modelled on Alfonso II, Duke of Ferrara, whose young wife, Lucrezia de’ Medici, died in 1561 under suspicious circumstances, possibly poisoned. Browning, however, does not write a historical documentary; he uses the historical setting to explore universal human passions. The form of the poem is a dramatic monologue. A dramatic monologue is a poem spoken by a single character — not the poet — to a silent listener, at a critical moment. The speaker reveals his own character, often unintentionally, through what he says and how he says it. In “My Last Duchess,” the Duke speaks to an emissary of a Count. The Duke is negotiating a new marriage, and during the conversation he shows the emissary a portrait of his last Duchess. As the Duke talks, he gradually reveals that he was responsible for her death. The poem is written in rhyming couplets of iambic pentameter, but Browning uses heavy enjambment (run-on lines), so the poem sounds like natural, dramatic speech rather than mechanical verse. The rhyme is often disguised by the sentence flowing over the line endings. The title is significant. The possessive “My” immediately tells us that the Duke thinks of the Duchess as a possession. The word “Last” is chilling: it implies that there have been earlier duchesses, and there will be more. The word also suggests that she is the latest in a series of owned objects. The Duchess is not present as a living woman; she is only a painting, an object on the wall. This study guide provides the complete text, a section-wise summary, detailed analysis, poetic devices, themes, and critical opinions. 2. Complete Text of the Poem

Which Claus of Innsbruck cast in bronze for me! 3. Section-Wise Summary The poem has no stanza breaks; it is a single unbroken verse paragraph. However, for convenience, we can divide it into six sections and summarise each. Section 1: Lines 1–13 — The Portrait and the Curtain The Duke points to the portrait of his last Duchess and invites the envoy to sit and look at it. He says the painting looks “as if she were alive.” He mentions that the artist was Frà Pandolf, a fictional painter. The Duke says he deliberately mentioned the artist’s name because visitors are always struck by the “depth and passion” of the Duchess’s glance in the portrait. They want to ask how such a glance came there, but they do not dare ask him directly. The Duke reveals that only he draws the curtain covering the portrait. This curtain is a powerful symbol of his control: he alone decides who can see the Duchess. The Duchess is no longer a free person; she is an object kept behind a curtain. Section 2: Lines 13–21 — The Blush and Frà Pandolf

The Duke explains that the “spot of joy” — the blush — on the Duchess’s cheek was not caused only by her husband’s presence. He suspects that Frà Pandolf may have paid her compliments while painting her, saying something like “Her mantle laps / Over my lady’s wrist too much,” or that paint can never capture the faint blush on her throat. The Duke says the Duchess thought such “courtesy” was enough to make her blush. This section already reveals the Duke’s jealousy. A simple blush from common courtesy is, for him, a sign of her too-warm, too-easy nature. Section 3: Lines 21–34 — The Duchess’s Universal Warmth The Duke complains that the Duchess had a heart “too soon made glad, / Too easily impressed.” She liked everything she looked on, and her looks went everywhere. He gives a list of things that gave her pleasure:  His favour at her breast — a gift from him, perhaps a jewel or ribbon.  The sunset.  A branch of cherries brought by “some officious fool.”  The white mule she rode. The Duke is offended that she valued all these things equally. A sunset, a cherry bough, a mule, and his gift all produced the same smile or blush. Worse, she thanked men as if she ranked his gift of a “nine-hundred-years-old name” — his aristocratic lineage — with anybody’s gift. This is the core of his anger: the Duchess did not treat him as superior. She was warm, democratic, and generous, but the Duke interprets this as a lack of discrimination and respect. Section 4: Lines 34–43 — The Duke’s Refusal to Stoop The Duke asks, “Who’d stoop to blame / This sort of trifling?” He imagines explaining his displeasure to her, telling her exactly what disgusted him. But even if he had the skill in speech — and he claims, falsely, that he does not — he would not do it. To explain himself would be “stooping.” The Duke says, “I choose / Never to stoop.” This is a moment of enormous dramatic irony. The Duke considers ordinary communication, correction, and compromise as beneath his dignity. His pride is absolute. He would rather destroy the Duchess than ask her to change her behaviour.

## Section 5: Lines 43–47 — The Murder

The Duke admits that the Duchess smiled whenever he passed, but she smiled the same way at everyone else. “This grew,” he says. Then comes the most chilling line of the poem: “I gave commands; / Then all smiles stopped together.” The Duke does not say “I killed her.” He uses a euphemism — “I gave commands” — and the result is “all smiles stopped together.” The reader understands that he ordered her death. The cold, indirect language is terrifying. He then returns to the portrait: “There she stands / As if alive.” The irony is unbearable: she is now only alive in paint, and the Duke prefers her this way — silent, still, and completely under his control. Section 6: Lines 47–56 — Return to Business and the Neptune Statue The Duke abruptly changes the subject. He asks the envoy to rise and meet the company below. He then returns to the marriage negotiation. He says the Count’s known generosity is enough to ensure that his request for a dowry will be accepted, though his real object is the Count’s daughter herself. As they go down, the Duke draws attention to another art object: a bronze statue of Neptune taming a sea-horse, made by Claus of Innsbruck. The statue is another symbol of power and control — Neptune, the god, controlling a wild creature. It parallels the Duke’s desire to control his wives. The

poem ends with the Duke still performing his role as a cultured aristocrat, but the reader has seen the violence beneath the surface. 4. Detailed Analysis 4.1 The Duke as a Dramatic Speaker The Duke is one of Browning’s most memorable villains because he is so polished, articulate, and utterly convinced of his own rightness. He speaks with elegance and control. He is polite — “Will’t please you sit and look at her?” — but his politeness is a mask. Every courtesy is also a command. He manipulates the envoy’s movements, the conversation, and the reader’s understanding. The Duke’s monologue is a self-revelation. He intends to show the envoy that he is a man of taste, wealth, and authority. Instead, he reveals himself as jealous, possessive, proud, and murderous. The gap between what the Duke says and what the reader understands is the poem’s central irony. 4.2 The Duchess as a Character The Duchess never appears in the poem as a living person. She is dead before the poem begins. She exists only in the Duke’s words and in the portrait. Yet Browning creates a vivid impression of her personality through the Duke’s complaints. She was warm, kind, easily delighted, and generous. She found joy in simple things: a sunset, a cherry bough, a ride on a mule. She thanked people. She smiled at everyone. She was, in other words, a naturally loving and egalitarian person. The Duke interprets these qualities as faults. She did not discriminate. She did not place her husband above others. She did not understand the weight of his nine-hundred-years-old name. The reader is likely to see the Duchess as innocent, warm, and lovable. The Duke’s criticism of her becomes an unintended tribute to her humanity. 4.3 Art and Objectification The poem is an example of ekphrasis — poetry about a work of art. The portrait of the Duchess is central. The Duke prefers the painted Duchess to the living woman. The painted Duchess is passive, silent, and entirely under his control. He can draw the curtain, reveal her, and hide her at will. The living Duchess was too independent, too free with her smiles, too ungovernable. The final image of Neptune taming a sea-horse reinforces this theme. Neptune, the god, controls a wild animal. The bronze statue is cold, hard, and permanent. The Duke loves art because it can be owned and controlled. He cannot control a living wife, but he can control a portrait. In this sense, the poem is a chilling meditation on the desire to turn people into objects. 4.4 The Irony of the Poem Browning uses dramatic irony throughout. The Duke believes he is presenting himself as a reasonable, cultured nobleman. The reader sees a murderer. The Duke calls the painting “a wonder,” but the real wonder is his own blindness to his cruelty. He says he has no skill in speech, yet his monologue is highly rhetorical and controlled. He says he chooses never to stoop, but he has stooped to the lowest possible act: murder.

There is also situational irony. The Duchess’s simple kindnesses, which should be virtues, become the cause of her death. The Duke’s aristocratic name, which he thinks is his greatest gift, is worthless to the Duchess because she values human warmth more than rank. The Duke kills her for being humane, then keeps her as a work of art. 4.5 The Ending The ending of the poem is masterful. After confessing — indirectly — that he ordered the Duchess’s death, the Duke immediately returns to the business of the dowry. He is not haunted by guilt. He does not pause. He is already thinking about his next wife. The mention of the statue of Neptune is not casual; it is a warning. The Duke is showing the envoy what happens when something wild is not tamed. The envoy now understands what kind of man the Count’s daughter will marry. The poem ends with the words “for me!” This final phrase captures the Duke’s egotism. Everything — the portrait, the statue, the Duchess, the new bride — exists for him. 5. Poetic Devices Browning uses a range of poetic devices to create the Duke’s voice and the poem’s effects. 5.1 Dramatic Monologue The poem is a classic dramatic monologue. It has:  A single speaker (the Duke).  A silent listener (the envoy).  A specific occasion (the marriage negotiation).  A revelatory moment (the confession of the murder). The reader is placed in the position of the silent listener, overhearing the Duke’s words and judging him. 5.2 Rhyming Couplets and Iambic Pentameter The poem is written in heroic couplets — pairs of rhyming lines in iambic pentameter. For example: “That’s my last Duchess painted on the wall,

## Looking as if she were alive. I call…”

The rhyme is regular, but Browning uses enjambment so frequently that the rhyme often goes unnoticed. The sentences flow from one line to the next, creating a conversational rhythm. The couplets give the poem a formal, controlled structure, which mirrors the Duke’s outward self-control. The enjambment, however, reveals the underlying pressure of his emotions. 5.3 Enjambment

Enjambment is the continuation of a sentence beyond the end of a line. Examples: “She had

## A heart—how shall I say?—too soon made glad,

Too easily impressed; she liked whate’er She looked on, and her looks went everywhere.” The run-on lines make the speech sound natural, but they also create a sense of the Duke’s restless, controlling mind moving from one thought to another. 5.4 Caesura Caesura is a pause within a line. Browning uses dashes, semicolons, and exclamation marks to create pauses: “A heart—how shall I say?—too soon made glad,” “She thanked men—good! but thanked / Somehow—I know not how—” These pauses mimic the Duke’s speech patterns, his hesitations, and his attempts to control his anger. 5.5 Euphemism

The most famous euphemism in the poem is: “I gave commands; / Then all smiles stopped together.” The Duke does not say he killed her. He uses indirect, formal language. The euphemism reveals his coldness and his belief that he did nothing wrong. It also makes the revelation more shocking because the reader must supply the horrifying truth. 5.6 Metaphor and Simile  Simile: “Looking as if she were alive” — the portrait is compared to a living person.  Metaphor: The “spot of joy” is a metaphor for the Duchess’s blush and her emotional warmth.  Symbolic metaphor: The Neptune statue is a metaphor for the Duke’s desire to tame and control. 5.7 Symbolism  The curtain: symbolises the Duke’s control over the Duchess’s image and memory.  The portrait: symbolises the Duchess as an object, frozen and owned.  The blush: symbolises the Duchess’s spontaneous warmth and humanity.  The white mule: symbolises simple, natural pleasures that the Duke despises.  Neptune taming a sea-horse: symbolises masculine power, domination, and the taming of wild nature. 5.8 Allusion

## The poem alludes to:

 Frà Pandolf: an invented Renaissance painter; the name gives an air of artistic culture.  Claus of Innsbruck: an invented sculptor; again a mark of the Duke’s connoisseurship.  Neptune: the Roman god of the sea, symbolising power and control. These allusions contribute to the historical Italian setting and to the Duke’s self-presentation as a cultured Renaissance nobleman. 5.9 Rhetorical Questions The Duke uses rhetorical questions to justify himself and to manipulate the listener: “Who’d stoop to blame / This sort of trifling?” The question expects no answer because the Duke assumes the envoy agrees with him. It also reveals his arrogance. 5.10 Irony The whole poem is built on irony. The Duke says one thing, but the reader understands another. For example, he says he has no skill in speech, but he speaks with great rhetorical skill. He says he chooses never to stoop, but he has stooped to murder. He calls the Duchess’s behaviour “trifling,” but his own pride is the real trifling matter. 6. Major Themes 6.1 Power and Control The Duke is obsessed with power. He controls the portrait with a curtain. He controls the conversation with the envoy. He controlled his wife by killing her. He is now negotiating for a new wife, whom he will also try to control. The poem shows that absolute power corrupts absolutely. The Duke’s power is not just political; it is personal and domestic. He cannot tolerate any independence in his wife. 6.2 Jealousy and Possessiveness The Duke is intensely jealous. He cannot bear that the Duchess smiled at others. He cannot bear that she thanked men. He cannot bear that she valued simple gifts as much as his name. His jealousy is not ordinary insecurity; it is the jealousy of an owner whose property is not behaving properly. The Duchess is not a person to him; she is a possession. 6.3 Gender and Patriarchy The poem is a powerful critique of patriarchal power. The Duke represents the male aristocrat who expects his wife to be submissive, silent, and obedient. The Duchess’s warmth and independence are seen as threats. Her murder is the ultimate expression of patriarchal violence. The poem exposes the cruelty behind the ideal of the obedient wife.

6.4 Class and Aristocratic Pride

The Duke’s pride is rooted in his class. He believes his “nine-hundred-years-old name” is the greatest gift he can give. He is insulted that the Duchess did not recognise this. The poem shows how class pride can dehumanise others. The Duke values rank more than love, kindness, or life itself. 6.5 Art versus Life The Duke prefers art to life because art can be controlled. The portrait of the Duchess is a “wonder” because it is static and owned. The living Duchess was too unpredictable. The Neptune statue is also a controlled image of wild nature. The poem suggests a dangerous aestheticism: the love of beauty can become a love of possession, and the desire to possess can lead to violence. 6.6 Appearance versus Reality The Duke presents himself as a refined, cultured gentleman. He speaks politely, appreciates art, and entertains the envoy. But beneath this appearance is a murderer. The poem teaches us to distrust appearances and to listen for the truth behind words. The Duke’s polished surface conceals a brutal reality. 6.7 Marriage as Transaction The poem also critiques the aristocratic marriage market. The Duke is negotiating a dowry with the Count’s envoy. His “object,” he says, is the Count’s daughter, but the dowry is also important. Marriage is a business arrangement, not a union of love. The Duchess’s own marriage to the Duke was presumably similar. The new bride will enter the same system of ownership and control. 7. Critical Opinions Critics have long admired “My Last Duchess” for its psychological depth and its mastery of the dramatic monologue.

## Robert Langbaum

The critic Robert Langbaum, in his influential book The Poetry of Experience (1957), argued that the dramatic monologue creates a tension between sympathy and judgment. The reader is drawn into the speaker’s point of view, but is also forced to judge the speaker morally. In “My Last Duchess,” Langbaum says, we are initially seduced by the Duke’s eloquence and charm, but we eventually recoil from his cruelty. The poem’s power lies in this double movement: we understand the Duke, but we do not forgive him.

## J. Hillis Miller

The American critic J. Hillis Miller has read the poem as a study in objectification. The Duke, Miller argues, transforms the Duchess from a living person into an aesthetic object. The portrait is the final stage of this transformation. The Duke is

not just a murderer; he is an artist of control. He has remade the Duchess into something he can possess. Miller connects this to a broader Victorian anxiety about the relationship between art and life.

## Isobel Armstrong

The critic Isobel Armstrong, in Victorian Poetry: Poetry, Poetics and Politics (1993), emphasises the poem’s gender politics. She argues that Browning’s dramatic monologue exposes the violence hidden in patriarchal marriage. The Duke speaks the language of ownership and control, and his murder of the Duchess is the logical end of that language. Armstrong sees the poem as a critique of Victorian gender ideology, using the Renaissance setting to make the critique more shocking.

## G. K. Chesterton

G. K. Chesterton, one of Browning’s early champions, praised the poem for its psychological realism. He noted that Browning could enter the mind of a villain without endorsing the villain’s views. The Duke is not a simple monster; he is a complex, believable human being whose evil is rooted in his pride and his sense of entitlement. Chesterton admired Browning’s ability to make evil understandable without making it attractive.

## T. S. Eliot

T. S. Eliot admired Browning’s use of the dramatic monologue, though he also noted that Browning sometimes did not go as far as modern poets in exploring the subconscious. Eliot saw in “My Last Duchess” a powerful example of how poetry can dramatise a character through speech. The Duke’s monologue, for Eliot, is not a lyrical outpouring but a dramatic action: the poem is something that happens, not something that is merely said. 8. Only he can reveal her. 3. “A heart—how shall I say?—too soon made glad, / Too easily impressed.” The Duke criticises the Duchess’s warmth and spontaneity. The reader sees these as virtues, not faults. 4. “as if she ranked / My gift of a nine-hundred-years-old name / With anybody’s gift.” This is the heart of the Duke’s grievance. His aristocratic pride is wounded because the Duchess treats everyone equally. 5. “I choose / Never to stoop.” The Duke’s motto. He will not lower himself to explain or negotiate. His pride is absolute. 6. “I gave commands; / Then all smiles stopped together.” The central confession. The Duke reveals, indirectly, that he ordered the Duchess’s death. 7. “Notice Neptune, though, / Taming a sea-horse, thought a rarity, / Which Claus of Innsbruck cast in bronze for me!” The final image of domination and possession. Conclusion “My Last Duchess” is a short poem, but it contains a whole world of psychological and moral complexity. Browning compresses a story of courtship, jealousy, murder, and remarriage into fifty-six lines. The Duke is one of literature’s most compelling villains because he is so plausible, so cultivated, and so blind to his own evil. The poem remains relevant because it explores timeless themes: the abuse of power, the objectification of women, the dangers of pride, and the relationship between art and life. For WBSU English Honours students, this poem is an essential text for understanding the Victorian dramatic monologue and Browning’s contribution to English poetry. A careful reading of the poem — paying attention to its form, language, and ironies — reveals why it has remained a classic for more than a century and a half.
