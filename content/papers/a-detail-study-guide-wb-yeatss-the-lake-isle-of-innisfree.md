## 1. Introduction and Context

"The Lake Isle of Innisfree" is one of W.B. Yeats's most celebrated and enduring early poems. Written in 1888 and published in his 1890 collection The Rose, it is a quintessential example of his early lyric style, steeped in Celtic Twilight romanticism and a deep yearning for escape. While Yeats's later work became increasingly complex, modernist, and politically charged, this poem remains beloved for its simple, hypnotic beauty and its universal theme of finding peace in nature.

For the WBSU syllabus, this poem is crucial for understanding Yeats's point of origin. It represents a pastoral, escapist impulse that he would later critique in himself. The poem was famously inspired by a moment of urban nostalgia: as a young man in London, Yeats heard the sound of a water fountain in a shop window on Fleet Street. This simple sound transported him back to his childhood in County Sligo, Ireland, and specifically to the uninhabited island of Innisfree in Lough Gill. He recalled his boyhood dream of living there in solitude, a dream that crystallized into this iconic poem.

## 2. The Complete Text

I will arise and go now, and go to Innisfree,  
And a small cabin build there, of clay and wattles made:  
Nine bean-rows will I have there, a hive for the honey-bee,  
And live alone in the bee-loud glade.  
And I shall have some peace there, for peace comes dropping slow,  
Dropping from the veils of the morning to where the cricket sings;  
There midnight’s all a glimmer, and noon a purple glow,  
And evening full of the linnet’s wings.  
I will arise and go now, for always night and day  
I hear lake water lapping with low sounds by the shore;  
While I stand on the roadway, or on the pavements grey,  
I hear it in the deep heart’s core.  

## 3. Stanza-wise Summary & Analysis

### Stanza 1: The Declaration of Intent

The poem begins with a powerful, almost biblical declaration: "I will arise and go now." This echoes the parable of the Prodigal Son ("I will arise and go to my father"), framing the journey not as a mere holiday, but as a spiritual homecoming. The speaker is not just visiting Innisfree; he is returning to a fundamental part of himself. His plan is simple and idyllic: build a small cabin of "clay and wattles" (a traditional Irish building method using woven sticks and mud) and cultivate "nine bean-rows" and a "hive for the honey-bee." The life he envisions is one of self-sufficiency and harmony with nature. The final image of the stanza, "live alone in the bee-loud glade," is a brilliant paradox. The "glade" (an open space in a wood) is a place of profound solitude, yet it is not silent; it is "loud" with the vibrant, life-affirming sound of bees. This is not a lonely isolation but a rich, purposeful solitude.

### Stanza 2: The Texture of Peace

The second stanza shifts from the what of his dream to the how it feels. The key word is "peace." It is not a dramatic event, but a slow, gentle process, "dropping slow" like dew or light rain. Yeats uses temporal imagery to paint a full sensory picture of a day on the island. The morning is soft, with peace dropping "from the veils of the morning." The noon is not harsh sunlight, but a mystical "purple glow." Midnight is not dark and frightening, but "all a glimmer" with starlight. The evening is alive with the fluttering of "linnet’s wings." This is a landscape bathed in a perpetual, magical twilight, a place outside of normal time where every moment is beautiful. The peace he craves is not an absence of sensation, but a saturation of it—a life attuned to the subtle rhythms of the natural world.

### Stanza 3: The Antithesis and the Heart’s Core

The final stanza returns to the opening refrain, "I will arise and go now," but this time the reason is explicit. The speaker is not on a hilltop overlooking the lake; he is "on the roadway, or on the pavements grey." This is the urban world of London, a world of monotony, colourlessness ("grey"), and alienation. The contrast is stark. The "purple glow" of Innisfree is the opposite of the "pavements grey." The most powerful image is saved for last: the "lake water lapping with low sounds by the shore." This auditory memory is not just a passing thought; it is embedded in "the deep heart’s core." The sound of Innisfree is the fundamental rhythm of his being, a constant, subconscious presence that cannot be silenced by the noise of the city. The poem thus moves from a conscious decision, to a sensory dream, to a profound, inner compulsion.

## 4. Poetic Devices and Craft

Yeats’s artistry is in making a complex emotional state feel simple and inevitable. He achieves this through masterful technique.

- **Rhythm and Meter:** The poem is written in a loose iambic hexameter (six beats per line), with the first and third lines of each stanza often being longer, creating a long, flowing, almost chant-like rhythm. This hypnotic rhythm mimics the "lapping" of the water he describes.
- **Alliteration and Assonance:** The soundscape of the poem is crucial. Alliteration abounds: "bean-rows," "hive for the honey-bee," "live alone in the bee-loud glade," "lake water lapping with low sounds." Assonance also plays a key role, such as the long 'o' sounds in "dropping slow" and "purple glow," which create a sense of slowness and openness.
- **Refrain:** The repetition of "I will arise and go now" in the first and third stanzas gives the poem a ritualistic, incantatory power. It transforms the desire from a simple wish into a solemn vow.
- **Synesthesia:** Yeats blends the senses seamlessly. We hear the "bee-loud glade," we see the "purple glow," and we feel the "peace... dropping slow." The most powerful example is the combination of sight and sound in the "lake water lapping," which becomes a tactile and emotional sensation in the heart.


## 5. Core Themes

- Escapism and the Antithesis of City and Country: The poem is a classic expression of the desire to flee the artificiality and stress of urban life for the authenticity and simplicity of nature. The "pavements grey" stand in direct opposition to the vibrant life of Innisfree.
- The Celtic Twilight and the Search for Spiritual Peace: As a young man, Yeats was deeply involved in the Celtic Revival, seeking to create a distinctly Irish literary identity rooted in folklore, myth, and the landscape. Innisfree is a sacred, almost mystical space in the Irish imagination. The peace he seeks is not just physical rest but a spiritual wholeness.
- **Memory and Imagination:** The poem is driven by the power of a memory—the sound of water. This memory acts as a catalyst for the imagination, which builds a perfect world in the mind. The real Innisfree is less important than the idea of Innisfree that the speaker carries within him.
- **The Power of Sound:** The poem is orchestrated around sound. From the bees to the crickets to the linnet’s wings to the lapping water, it is sound that defines the experience of the island and that ultimately haunts the speaker in the city. It is an audible memory that speaks to the "deep heart’s core."


## 6. Critics' Opinions & Scholarly Perspectives

To elevate your analysis beyond simple appreciation, engaging with critical perspectives is essential.

- **Harold Bloom:** Bloom, the influential American critic, often viewed this poem with a degree of impatience typical of his focus on Yeats's later, more intellectually fraught work. He might consider it part of the "poetry of longing" that Yeats had to outgrow. However, he acknowledges its perfect execution of its aims, calling it a "beautiful, minor lyric" that captures a universal human desire.
- **Seamus Heaney:** The Nobel laureate and fellow Irish poet offered a profound and sympathetic reading. Heaney saw the poem not as a piece of naive escapism, but as a "psychic force-field." He argued that the poem's power lies in its ability to make the imagined world feel more real than the physical one. Heaney praised the "lunging, iambic" rhythm, which he felt physically enacted the speaker’s yearning.
- A. Norman Jeffares: Jeffares, a foundational Yeats scholar, provides essential biographical context. He connects the poem directly to Yeats’s youth in Sligo and his reading of Thoreau’s Walden, which advocated for a simple life in nature. This biographical grounding helps students see the poem as a deeply personal expression of a real longing, not just a generic Romantic trope.

> "I will arise and go now, and go to Innisfree,  
> And a small cabin build there, of clay and wattles made:  
> Nine bean-rows will I have there, a hive for the honey-bee,  
> And live alone in the bee-loud glade."  


**Context:** These are the opening lines of W.B. Yeats’s early lyric, "The Lake Isle of Innisfree," published in 1890. The poem is a powerful expression of the speaker’s deep-seated desire to escape the grey monotony of urban life in London and return to the idyllic, natural landscape of his Irish childhood. This first stanza establishes the speaker's intention and outlines his simple, self-sufficient plan for life on the island.

**Explanation:** The stanza is built upon a powerful, decisive declaration: "I will arise and go now." This phrase echoes the biblical story of the Prodigal Son, imbuing the journey with a sense of spiritual urgency and homecoming. The speaker’s architectural and agricultural plans are deliberately humble, reflecting a profound desire for authenticity. The "clay and wattles" cabin is a traditional Irish dwelling, signifying a return to one's roots. The specificity of "nine bean-rows" and a "hive for the honey-bee" creates a tangible, almost practical vision of his life, grounding the dream in reality. This is a life of purposeful labour, lived in direct harmony with the earth. The stanza culminates in the brilliant paradox of the "bee-loud glade." Here, solitude is not silence. The "glade" promises privacy and seclusion, but it is filled with the vibrant, constant hum of bees—a sound that represents life, industry, and the natural world’s ceaseless energy.

**Critical Analysis:** This opening captures the essence of what critic Seamus Heaney identified as the poem’s "psychic force-field." The language is so direct and rhythmic, and the imagery so concrete, that the imagined world of Innisfree begins to feel more substantial to the reader than the real world. The iambic rhythm, with its strong, rising beats on "arise" and "go now," creates a sense of irrepressible momentum. The speaker is not merely daydreaming; he is enacting a resolution. From a post-colonial perspective, one could also argue that the return to "clay and wattles" and traditional agriculture is a form of cultural reclamation, a turning away from the industrialized, imperial centre (London) towards a pre-colonial, pastoral Irish identity. The "bee-loud glade" becomes a symbol of an autonomous and thriving Irish space, free from English influence, resonating with the ideals of the Celtic Revival.

> "And I shall have some peace there, for peace comes dropping slow,  
> Dropping from the veils of the morning to where the cricket sings;  
> There midnight’s all a glimmer, and noon a purple glow,  
> And evening full of the linnet’s wings."  


**Context:** This is the second stanza of Yeats's "The Lake Isle of Innisfree." Having declared his intention to leave for the island in the first stanza, the speaker now elaborates on the fundamental reason for his journey: the pursuit of "peace." This stanza moves beyond the physical details of the island to describe its unique, almost magical, temporal and sensory atmosphere.

**Explanation:** The central idea here is the nature of peace. Yeats defines it not as an active pursuit, but as a passive reception; it "comes dropping slow." This simile suggests a gentle, natural, and inevitable process, like dewfall or gentle rain. The peace is not a single entity but a quality that pervades the entire day. It "drops from the veils of the morning," linking the abstract feeling to the tangible mist of dawn. The stanza then charts a full day on the island, transforming each part of it into something beautiful and magical. Noon is not harsh or glaring, but possesses a "purple glow," a colour associated with royalty, spirituality, and mystery. Midnight is not a time of darkness or fear, but is "all a glimmer," filled with the soft light of stars. The day ends with an evening alive with the quiet, frantic motion of "the linnet’s wings." The speaker paints a picture of a world in a state of perpetual, ethereal beauty, far removed from the clock-driven time of the city.

**Critical Analysis:** This stanza showcases Yeats’s masterful use of imagery and his debt to the Symbolist movement. The colours—purple, glimmer—are not meant to be literal but are symbolic, evocative of a heightened, almost visionary state. The "linnet's wings" and "cricket sings" contribute to the poem’s rich soundscape, emphasizing that this peace is not an absence of life but a harmonious integration with it. Critics like Harold Bloom might see this as the height of Yeats’s "poetry of longing," a beautiful but ultimately youthful fantasy. However, one could argue that Yeats is not just describing a place, but a mode of perception. The peace he seeks is the ability to see the "purple glow" in the noon and the "glimmer" at midnight; it is a state of being where the world is revealed as enchanted. This idea aligns with the Celtic Twilight movement, which sought to rediscover the magic and folklore embedded in the Irish landscape as a counter to modern, rationalist, and industrial despair.

> "I will arise and go now, for always night and day  
> I hear lake water lapping with low sounds by the shore;  
> While I stand on the roadway, or on the pavements grey,  
> I hear it in the deep heart’s core."  


**Context:** These are the concluding lines of W.B. Yeats's "The Lake Isle of Innisfree." The stanza marks a return to the opening refrain, but with a crucial shift in emphasis. It reveals the immediate, pressing reason for the speaker's decision to leave, contrasting the perpetual, haunting call of Innisfree with the grim reality of his current existence in the city.

**Explanation:** The refrain "I will arise and go now" is repeated, but now it is followed by "for," introducing a causal explanation. The speaker’s desire is not a fleeting whim; it is a constant, unrelenting compulsion. He hears the "lake water lapping with low sounds by the shore" not occasionally, but "always night and day." This sound is an auditory hallucination, a memory so potent it has become a lived reality. The poem’s central antithesis is drawn with sharp precision in the next two lines. The speaker is physically located "on the roadway, or on the pavements grey." This is the grimy, monotonous, and alienating world of the modern city, symbolized by the stark, colourless adjective "grey." It is the absolute opposite of the vibrant, mystical colours and sounds of Innisfree. The final line is the poem’s emotional climax. The sound of the water is not heard with the ear, but with the "deep heart’s core." This phrase suggests that the longing for Innisfree is not superficial nostalgia, but a fundamental part of his identity, his very soul.

**Critical Analysis:** This stanza is the engine of the entire poem. It transforms the fantasy from a pleasant daydream into an existential necessity. The city is not just unpleasant; it is fundamentally antithetical to his being. The greyness of the pavement represents a spiritual death, a life of conformity and alienation, which reflects the anxieties of the modern, industrialized world. Seamus Heaney’s reading is particularly insightful here. He argued that the poem is about the power of the imagination to create a "force-field" so strong that it can dominate physical reality. The speaker may be standing in London, but his true psychic location is Innisfree. The "deep heart’s core" is the seat of his authentic self, which remains irrevocably connected to the Irish landscape. The poem, therefore, is not just about escaping the city, but about the inability of the city to ever fully claim the soul of the poet. It is a testament to the enduring power of one’s origins and the imagined homeland.

**Context:** This RTC requires a thematic linking of two distinct parts of Yeats's "The Lake Isle of Innisfree": the practical vision of the first stanza and the profound, internalized conclusion of the last. Together, they encapsulate the poem’s entire arc, moving from a detailed plan for a simple life in nature to a deep, spiritual compulsion rooted in the speaker's identity. **Explanation:** The first quotation represents the poem's conscious, practical surface. The "nine bean-rows" and "hive for the honey-bee" are symbols of a self-sufficient, deliberate life. It reveals that this planned solitude is not meant to be lonely or empty. The paradox of "bee-loud" suggests a rich, vibrant aloneness, where the individual is alone with, and becomes a part of, the natural world’s chorus. By the final stanza, this conscious plan has been supplanted by something far more powerful. The "deep heart’s core" is the realm of the subconscious, of instinct, and of fundamental being. The desire to go to Innisfree is no longer just a good idea; it is who he is. The sound of the lake water is not just a pleasant memory, but a "low sound" that defines the rhythm of his very existence, a constant, underlying hum beneath the noise of urban life.

**Critical Analysis:** The journey between these two points is the poem's true subject. It is a movement from the conscious to the unconscious, from a plan to a prayer. This is precisely why the poem has such enduring power. It does not simply state a preference for the country over the city; it dramatizes the process by which a place can become embedded in one’s soul. A biographical critic like A. Norman Jeffares would point to Yeats’s own life, his boyhood in Sligo, and his reading of Thoreau, as the genesis of this feeling. However, the poem transcends biography. It speaks to the universal human experience of being psychically anchored to a "somewhere" that is more real than the place we physically inhabit. The final line is not a conclusion but a revelation: the speaker is already at Innisfree, in his heart. The physical journey is secondary; the spiritual connection is primary and unbreakable. The poem is a testament to the idea that our truest home is not a geographical location, but a state of the soul.
